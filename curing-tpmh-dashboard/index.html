Latest improvement — Time-of-Shift Robustness Gate

A 12-hour shift average can hide deterioration in the later part of the shift. Keep the existing workload-state method, but tag every baseline/shadow interval with hours since shift start and compare early/middle/late portions.

Compare: NORMAL/CONSTRAINED/SATURATED/RECOVERY minutes, backlog/carryover, break completion/interruptions, outside-support people-minutes, response/recovery, short-interval production loss, and observed walking/manual-work burden.
Rule: do not invent a fatigue threshold and do not assume degradation. Look for a repeatable time-of-shift pattern across comparable exposures.
If a pattern appears: investigate workload, break timing/quality, motion, ergonomic/environmental demand, staffing/relief, equipment mix and accumulated recovery debt before accepting 2-op.

Best current strategy

  1. Remove avoidable motion and point-of-use gaps.
  2. Demand-weight Op1/Op2 zones by work + travel + abnormal exposure.
  3. Build variability-aware reserve and collision exposure.
  4. Apply qualification redundancy / pair robustness.
  5. Freeze NORMAL routes and single-owner ABNORMAL dispatch.
  6. Use visual abnormality ownership plus NORMAL → CONSTRAINED → SATURATED → RECOVERY states.
  7. Protect due work with a Protected Work Matrix + Due-Time Recovery Queue.
  8. Use qualified break relief and account for relief debt/home-role recovery.
  9. Use leader standard work, process confirmation, scenario challenge and readiness gate.
  10. Collect matched 3-op baseline and short-interval capacity-loss data.
  11. Run controlled shadow 2-op with rescue-only third operator.
  12. Calculate TPMH, Effective Labor, Support Burden and Adjusted TPMH.
  13. Compare stable-condition segments for workload state, collisions, support, relief debt and recovery.
  14. Apply Time-of-Shift Robustness Gate across the 12-hour operating window.
  15. Pareto exceptions, redesign and repeat across representative A/B/C/D conditions before true 2-op.
